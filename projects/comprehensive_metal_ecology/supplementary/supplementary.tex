\section{Gene list construction and evidence tiers}
\label{sm:genelist}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Candidate KO universe}

The metal-gene list was assembled from three independent sources:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{KEGG ORTHOLOGY / BRITE hierarchies.} KOs classified under BRITE
  modules for heavy-metal resistance, metal transport, and metalloenzymes were
  downloaded in 2024.  Only KOs with an explicit metal annotation in the KEGG
  reaction or definition field were retained.

\item \textbf{BacMet2 database}~\cite{pal2014}.  Experimentally validated
  metal resistance genes from BacMet v2.0.  KEGG IDs were assigned by mapping
  BacMet gene names to KEGG gene identifiers via the KEGG REST API
  (\texttt{/find/genes/\{name\}} endpoint).

\item \textbf{FitnessBrowser}~\cite{price2018}.  Genome-wide transposon
  fitness screens under metallic stress conditions.  KOs with significant
  fitness defects ($|\text{fitness score}| > 1.5$, $|t| > 5$) under $\geq 1$
  metal stressor were included.
\end{enumerate}

KOs appearing in $\geq 2$ sources were cross-validated; those from only one
source were retained as lower-confidence candidates with appropriate tier labels.
The final list covers 730 KOs across 17 metals.

\subsection{Evidence tiers}

Each of the 730 candidate KOs was assigned to one of five evidence tiers:

\textbf{Primary KO set} used in all confirmatory analyses: Tier~1 + Tier~2
= \textbf{140 KOs}.  These have the strongest multi-source evidence and
unambiguous KEGG functional annotations.  The full list with curation
rationale is provided in Supplementary Table~S2.

\subsection{Functional categories}

Each KO was assigned a primary functional category based on KEGG annotation
and BacMet2 gene function:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Transport / Homeostasis} — metal importers, exporters,
  metallochaperones, siderophore-uptake systems, and ferritins.

\item \textbf{Resistance / Detoxification} — metal-efflux pumps, detoxification
  enzymes, and metal-responsive regulatory proteins that confer resistance.

\item \textbf{Sensing / Regulation} — two-component sensor kinases, MerR-family
  metalloregulators, and other metal-sensing regulatory elements.

\item \textbf{Cofactor Biosynthesis} — enzymes in Fe--S cluster assembly,
  molybdopterin (Moco) biosynthesis, and cobalamin pathway steps that
  incorporate metal cofactors.

\item \textbf{Metal-dependent Metabolism} — metabolic enzymes whose catalytic
  activity requires a metal cofactor, supported by KEGG module assignment and
  fitness screen evidence.
\end{itemize}

Assignment is based on the most specific category for each KO;
\texttt{overlap\_flag = True} identifies six KOs with dual-function ambiguity
in the full 730-KO list (not in the primary 140-KO set).

\subsection{Metal coverage}

The primary 140-KO set covers 17 metals: Fe, Zn, Co, Mn, Cu, Ni, S, Mo, Cd,
Al, Tl, Ag, Hg, Au, Pb, As, and Bi.  Metal annotations are listed per KO in
Supplementary Table~S2.  Metal-specific PGLS subsets were defined by filtering
to KOs whose \texttt{metals} field contains the target metal.  Nine metals with
$\geq 34$ primary KOs were included in the metal-specific confirmatory analysis:
Co (101 KOs), Fe (98), Ni (84), S (74), Cu (71), Zn (67), Tl (45), Al (34),
Mn (34). For Al, Tl, and S --- elements with fewer dedicated resistance systems --- KOs were assigned based on fitness-screen evidence or documented biochemical function; full curation rationale for these three metals is provided in Supplementary Table~S4.

\subsection{Pfam / InterPro structural QC}

To assess whether the primary KO set has independent structural evidence of
metal interaction, we performed a three-step API pipeline for all 140 KOs:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{KO $\to$ gene:} KEGG REST \texttt{link/genes/ko:\{KO\}} filtered
  to representative model organisms in priority order (\textit{E.\ coli},
  \textit{B.\ subtilis}, \textit{P.\ aeruginosa}, \textit{Synechocystis},
  \textit{M.\ tuberculosis}, \textit{S.\ coelicolor}, \textit{R.\ prowazekii},
  \textit{C.\ jejuni}, \textit{B.\ pertussis}, \textit{M.\ genitalium});
  then \texttt{conv/uniprot/\{gene\_id\}} to obtain a UniProt accession.
  Rate: 0.4\,s per call; 3-retry exponential back-off.

\item \textbf{Gene $\to$ Pfam:} UniProt REST
  \texttt{/\{acc\}?fields=xref\_pfam\&format=tsv} to retrieve Pfam domain IDs.

\item \textbf{Pfam $\to$ clan:} InterPro API
  \texttt{/entry/pfam/\{PF\}/?format=json} $\to$
  \texttt{metadata.set\_info.accession} to identify the InterPro clan.
  Rate: 0.3\,s per call.
\end{enumerate}

Metal-binding domains were defined by five InterPro clans (verified in
InterPro version 97+): CL0704 (HMA heavy-metal-associated domain), CL0344
(4Fe--4S ferredoxin), CL0486 (Fer2 / 2Fe--2S ferredoxin), CL0361 (C2H2 zinc
finger), and CL0193 (MBB metal beta-barrel); plus $\approx$25 metal-binding
singleton Pfams identified from literature.

\textbf{Results:} 10/140 (7.1\%) KOs have $\geq 1$ metal-binding clan or
singleton Pfam.  The 130 without clan evidence fall into three classes:
\texttt{pfam\_no\_metal\_clan} (113 KOs — Pfam domains found but none in
metal-binding clans; predominantly ABC transporter ATPase / permease scaffolds
PF00005 / PF00950 / PF01032, MerR-family sensors, and outer-membrane efflux
proteins); \texttt{no\_gene\_found} (15 KOs — no representative bacterial gene
in KEGG REST); and \texttt{no\_pfam\_domains} (2 KOs: ARN / K08197 and cbiL /
K16915).  The low InterPro coverage is expected: metal transporters act via
substrate selectivity at the binding pocket rather than via catalytic
metal-cofactor domains, and will systematically fail clan-based Pfam filtering
(see also Fig.~S14 and §5.1 of the main text).

\section{Per-megabase KO density calculation}
\label{sm:density}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Genomic data sources}

\textbf{Primary dataset (bacteria, $n = 1{,}574$ genera):} BERDL Spark cluster,
namespace \texttt{kbase.ke\_pangenome}.  The \texttt{genus\_clusters} table
contains one row per GTDB genus with pangenome statistics (total gene count,
mean genome size in bp, and the full KO annotation set across all genomes in
the genus-level cluster).  Phylum / kingdom annotations joined from
\texttt{kbase.ke\_pangenome.gtdb\_taxonomy\_r214v1}.

\textbf{Geochemical replication dataset (soil concentration,
$n = 482$ genera):} \texttt{refdata.ngsa} (Australian National Geochemical
Survey of Australia; Geoscience Australia).  KO content extracted by the same
pipeline, restricted to genera detected in Australian soil and sediment samples.
Soil metal concentrations (ICP-MS, mg\,kg$^{-1}$) joined by a 200\,km radius
spatial join between NGSA sampling sites and genus occurrences.

\subsection{KO density formula}

For each genus $g$:

\begin{equation}
\text{ko\_per\_mb\_primary}(g) =
  \frac{n_{\text{distinct primary KOs}}(g)}{\text{mean\_genome\_size\_Mb}(g)}
\end{equation}

where $\text{mean\_genome\_size\_Mb} = \text{mean\_genome\_size\_bp} / 10^6$
averaged across all genomes in the genus cluster, and
$n_{\text{distinct primary KOs}}$ counts unique KO IDs from the 140-KO primary
set present in $\geq 1$ genome of the genus.

\subsection{Predictor standardisation}

For PGLS, \texttt{ko\_per\_mb\_primary} is $z$-scored within each analysis
dataset:
\begin{equation}
\text{predictor\_z} = \frac{\text{ko\_per\_mb\_primary} -
  \mu(\text{ko\_per\_mb\_primary})}{\sigma(\text{ko\_per\_mb\_primary})}
\end{equation}

Standardisation is performed separately within each analysis dataset
(bacteria, archaea, geochemical replication), so $\beta$ values are not
directly comparable across datasets.

\section{Ecological niche breadth quantification}
\label{sm:niche}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Levins' standardised niche breadth}

Niche breadth was quantified using Levins' $B$~\cite{levins1968}, standardised
following Colwell \& Futuyma~\cite{colwell1971}:

\begin{equation}
B = \frac{1}{\sum_i p_i^2} \qquad\quad
B_\mathrm{std} = \frac{B - 1}{n - 1}
\end{equation}

where $p_i$ is the proportional abundance of a genus in sample $i$ (relative
to total abundance of that genus across all samples) and $n$ is the total
number of samples.  $B_\mathrm{std} \in [0,1]$: 0 = complete specialist
(all occurrences in one sample), 1 = complete generalist (perfectly even
distribution).  Implementation: \texttt{scripts/niche\_utils.py::levins\_b\_std()}.

\subsection{Primary niche breadth dataset (MicrobeAtlas)}

\textbf{Data source:} \texttt{arkinlab.microbeatlas} (BERDL Spark) — genus-level
OTU abundance matrix across $>400{,}000$ global 16S rDNA amplicon samples
aggregated in the MicrobeAtlas database~\cite{sunagawa2015}.  Each OTU linked to a
genus via \texttt{otu\_pangenome\_link\_v2.csv}; per-genus $B_\mathrm{std}$
computed as the mean across all OTUs assigned to that genus.

\textbf{Minimum sample filter:} Genera detected in $<5$ samples are excluded.
Sensitivity checks at $n_\mathrm{min} = 50$ and $n_\mathrm{min} = 150$
produce identical results (analyses S4 and S5, Table S2 of the main text),
confirming that most genera in the dataset exceed these thresholds.

\subsection{EMP niche breadth (secondary, exploratory)}

\textbf{Data source:} Earth Microbiome Project 16S data
(\texttt{refdata.emp\_16s}).  EMPO (Earth Microbiome Project Ontology)
level-3 habitat categories used as the niche axis; level-2 used as fallback
when $<5$ distinct EMPO-3 categories were present for a genus.  Levins
$B_\mathrm{std}$ computed from the genus $\times$ EMPO-category co-occurrence
matrix.  Minimum: $\geq 5$ EMP samples per genus.  $n = 539$ genera after
merge with primary KO density data.

\textbf{Result:} $\lambda = 0.055$ (little phylogenetic signal in EMP niche
breadth), $\beta = -0.019$, $\text{SE} = 0.0115$, $p = 0.099$ (non-significant,
directionally consistent; see Fig.~S13).

\subsection{Soil-specialist definition}

For the soil-restricted sensitivity analysis (analysis S7 of the main text),
genera were classified as soil specialists via MicrobeAtlas \texttt{Env\_Level\_1}
using ecosystem categories: \textit{soil}, \textit{agricultural}, \textit{farm},
\textit{field}, \textit{paddy}, \textit{peatland}, \textit{desert}, \textit{shrub}.
Procedure: (1) identify the dominant \texttt{Env\_Level\_1} category per OTU
(row with maximum \texttt{n\_samples\_detected}); (2) flag OTU as
\texttt{is\_soil} if dominant environment $\in$ SOIL\_ENVS; (3) join OTUs to
genera; (4) compute $\text{frac\_soil} = n_\text{soil OTUs} / n_\text{OTUs}$
per genus; (5) classify genus as soil specialist if $\text{frac\_soil} > 0.5$.
Soil-restricted dataset: $n = 162$ genera.

\section{Phylogenetic tree}
\label{sm:tree}
% ============================================================================

\textbf{Source:} GTDB (Genome Taxonomy Database) Release 214v1~\cite{parks2022}.  The full
genus-level phylogeny was downloaded in Newick format and pruned to retain only
genera present in the PGLS input dataset using
\texttt{dendropy.Tree.prune\_taxa\_with\_labels()}.

\begin{itemize}
\item Bacterial tree: \texttt{data/gtdb\_bac\_genus\_pruned.tree}
  (2,283 taxa before pruning; 1,574 retained for the primary analysis).

\item Archaeal tree: \texttt{data/gtdb\_arc\_genus\_pruned.tree}
  (pruned to 95 genera for the archaeal replication; 41 for the archaeal
  sensitivity analysis at $n_\text{min} = 5$ genera per taxon).
\end{itemize}

Tip labels use lowercase genus names with spaces replaced by underscores,
matching the \texttt{genus\_lower} column in all data files.  All branch
lengths are in units of substitutions per site (molecular phylogram;
root-to-tip distances range from 0.886 to 2.994 substitutions per site,
confirming the tree is \textbf{not ultrametric}).

\textbf{Note on ultrametric assumption.} PGLS under Brownian motion
theoretically assumes an ultrametric (time-calibrated) phylogeny.  The GTDB
bacterial tree is a molecular phylogram, not a time-scaled tree, and this is a
limitation acknowledged here.  Two features of our implementation mitigate the
impact: (1) Pagel's $\lambda$ is estimated by maximum likelihood for each model,
which scales off-diagonal covariances and partially corrects for the
non-ultrametric structure~\cite{pagel1999}; (2) sensitivity analyses confirm
that the primary result holds at $\lambda = 0$ (OLS; $\beta = -0.032$,
$p \approx 0$) and $\lambda = 1$ (full Brownian motion; $\beta = -0.018$,
$p = 3.7 \times 10^{-6}$), spanning the range from ``tree structure ignored''
to ``full BM''.  The Grafen (1989) transformation to an arbitrary ultrametric
tree was not applied; this is a future robustness direction.

The circular cladogram of the bacterial tree coloured by KO density and niche
breadth is shown in Fig.~S1.

\section{Phylogenetic generalised least squares}
\label{sm:pgls}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Implementation}

All PGLS models were fitted using \texttt{scripts/pgls\_utils.py}, a custom
implementation using \texttt{dendropy}~5.0.8 for tree operations and
\texttt{scipy}~1.17.1~\cite{virtanen2020} / \texttt{numpy}~2.4.4 for
optimisation and linear algebra. All analyses were run in Python~3.13.9
(conda-forge, GCC~14.3.0). CatBoost (version 1.2) was used for the
exploratory LOPO analysis (SM~11). No R packages were used in the primary
confirmatory analyses.

\textbf{Variance--covariance matrix.}
Built from the pruned GTDB tree as $V[i,j] = $ shared branch length from root
to MRCA$(i,j)$ (= root-to-tip path length for $i = j$).

\textbf{Pagel's $\lambda$.}
Off-diagonal elements of $V$ are scaled by $\lambda \in (0,1)$:
\begin{equation}
V_\lambda[i,j] = \lambda \cdot V[i,j] \quad (i \neq j); \qquad
V_\lambda[i,i] = V[i,i]
\end{equation}
$\lambda = 0$: OLS (no phylogenetic signal); $\lambda = 1$: Brownian motion
(full phylogenetic signal).  $\lambda$ is estimated by maximising the profile
log-likelihood:
\begin{equation}
\log L = -\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[n\log(2\pi) + n\log(\sigma^2) +
  \log|V_\lambda| +
  (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^\top
  V_\lambda^{-1}
  (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) / \sigma^2\bigr]
\end{equation}
Optimisation via \texttt{scipy.optimize.minimize\_scalar()} with bounds
$(10^{-4},\, 1 - 10^{-4})$ and \texttt{method='bounded'}.

\textbf{GLS fit.}
After $\lambda$ optimisation, GLS coefficients $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ and
standard errors are estimated:
\begin{align}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} &=
  (\mathbf{X}^\top V^{-1} \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top V^{-1} \mathbf{y}
\\[4pt]
\sigma^2 &=
  \frac{(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^\top
    V_\lambda^{-1}
    (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{n - p}
\\[4pt]
\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j) &=
  \sqrt{\bigl[(\mathbf{X}^\top V^{-1} \mathbf{X})^{-1}\bigr]_{jj} \cdot \sigma^2}
\end{align}
where $p$ is the number of predictors including the intercept.

\textbf{Model statistics:}
$t_j = \hat{\beta}_j / \mathrm{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)$ with $\mathrm{df} = n - p$;
$\mathrm{AIC} = -2\log L + 2(p + 1)$;
$\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = \mathrm{AIC}(\text{model}) - \mathrm{AIC}(\text{null})$;
$r^2 = 1 - \mathrm{Var}(\text{residuals}) / \mathrm{Var}(\text{response})$.

\section{Multiple testing correction}
\label{sm:mtc}
% ============================================================================

All corrections use the Benjamini--Hochberg (BH) procedure.

\begin{description}
\item[Primary three-way replication (§2.1 of main text).]
  P-values from the three primary confirmatory tests (bacteria, archaea,
  geochemical) corrected jointly ($n = 3$).
  FDR: bacteria $= 4.28 \times 10^{-8}$; archaea $= 0.178$;
  geochemical $= 0.755$.

\item[Evidence-tier comparisons (§2.2 of main text).]
  Two tier comparisons (\texttt{all\_non\_ambiguous} and \texttt{bacmet\_only})
  corrected together ($n = 2$).

\item[Functional category comparisons (§3 of main text).]
  Five functional category PGLS models corrected together ($n = 5$).

\item[Metal-specific analyses (§4 of main text).]
  Nine metal-specific PGLS models corrected together ($n = 9$);
  all nine pass FDR $< 0.05$.

\item[Clade-stratified analyses.]
  Four phylum-stratified PGLS models corrected together ($n = 4$).
\end{description}

No multiple testing correction was applied to pre-specified confounder checks
(§5.2 of the main text) or to the eight sensitivity analyses (§5.3), as these
are robustness tests of a single primary result rather than independent
hypotheses. This design choice is pre-registered: confounder checks each ask
``does the primary result survive after accounting for variable $X$?''---they
test whether $\beta_\text{primary}$ is robust, not whether each confounder is
independently associated with niche breadth. Applying BH-FDR across confounder
checks would be statistically incorrect because the checks share the same null
hypothesis ($H_0$: primary $\beta = 0$) and the confounder itself is not the
quantity of interest. The split-magnitude permutation test (Section~7 of the
main text) is treated as an independent hypothesis test and is therefore the
sole focus of the confirmatory analysis family.

\section{Confounder analyses}
\label{sm:confounders}
% ============================================================================

For each potential confounder, we refitted the PGLS model adding the confounder
as a second predictor alongside \texttt{predictor\_z} and recorded the change
in the primary $\beta$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\beta\,(\%) =
  \frac{|\hat{\beta}_{\text{with confounder}} - \hat{\beta}_{\text{baseline}}|}
       {|\hat{\beta}_{\text{baseline}}|}
  \times 100
\end{equation}
\textbf{Decision rule:} $\Delta\beta > 50\%$ \textsc{and} $p > 0.05$ for the
density predictor $\Rightarrow$ confounder explains the effect.

\section{Latitude mechanism tests}
\label{sm:latitude}
% ============================================================================

The latitude amplification result (adding mean absolute latitude as a covariate
increases $|\beta|$ from $-0.021$ to $-0.031$, §5.2 of main text) motivated a
set of nine mechanistic tests examining whether a specific environmental
variable could explain the latitudinal gradient in niche breadth.  These are
exploratory analyses.

Models A--I each added a single environmental predictor alongside latitude to
the primary PGLS, testing whether the latitude effect was absorbed by:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Model A (bedrock Cr composite)}: GeoROC lithospheric
  metal-index principal component (driven by Cr: BH $p = 6.7 \times 10^{-9}$,
  and Co: BH $p = 0.0084$).

\item \textbf{Model B (temperature range)}: CHELSA annual temperature range
  (°C).

\item \textbf{Model C--H}: Ore-deposit proximity; bioavailable metal mobility
  index; soil metal hazard (CSU classification); soil temperature; lithology
  class; REE deposit proximity.

\item \textbf{Model I (soil organic matter)}: SOM (\%) as a redox proxy.
  \textbf{Key result:} SOM is an independent negative predictor
  ($\beta = -0.016$, $p = 6.8 \times 10^{-5}$); absorbs the pH association
  (pH becomes non-significant when SOM is included), consistent with
  organic-matter-driven metal speciation and bioavailability.

\item \textbf{Redox controls}: Soil moisture and SOM do not attenuate the
  Cr or Co bedrock signals ($p = 0.44$ and $0.50$ for attenuation tests),
  arguing against a waterlogging / soil-reduction mechanism for the serpentine
  Cr effect.
\end{itemize}

All 28 extended models are in \texttt{data/latitude\_mechanism\_results.csv}.

\section{Exploratory CatBoost LOPO analysis}
\label{sm:catboost}
% ============================================================================

\textit{These analyses are exploratory and carry lower inferential weight than the
pre-registered confirmatory analyses H1--H6.}

\subsection{Design and software}

CatBoost gradient-boosted trees (v1.2.10)~\cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost} were applied in a
Leave-One-Phylum-Out (LOPO) cross-validation design.
LOPO held out all genera from one phylum at a time across 11 phyla
(Proteobacteria 660, Firmicutes 330, Actinobacteria 203, Bacteroidetes 180,
Cyanobacteria 27, Planctomycetes 17, Chloroflexi 15, Verrucomicrobia 14,
Spirochaetes 11, Synergistetes 11, Acidobacteria 10 genera),
fitting on the remaining genera and evaluating predictions on held-out genera
using Spearman~$\rho$.
Model hyperparameters: \texttt{iterations=200, depth=4, learning\_rate=0.05,
l2\_leaf\_reg=3.0, thread\_count=1, random\_seed=42}.
\texttt{thread\_count=1} is required on the 128-core analysis server to
prevent CatBoost from consuming all cores via implicit parallelism; the
environment variable \texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS=1} was set at launch.

\subsection{Feature construction}

KO presence records from \texttt{data/nb25\_ko\_presence\_matrix.parquet}
(118 Tier~1+2 KOs with $\geq\!20$ genera each) were pivoted to a
genus $\times$ KO matrix, converted to per-Mb density (count of genomes with
KO / mean genome Mb), and $z$-scored across genera.
Genome size (\texttt{genome\_size\_mb\_z}) was included as a covariate in all
models.
Functional subset $z$-scores (12 predictors) were used in the subset-level
models; KO-to-subset assignments are from \texttt{data/s8\_ko\_metadata.csv}.

\subsection{Environmental responses}

Fifteen environmental $z$-scores were used as response variables:
six CSU mobile metal fractions (PF1$_{\mathrm{As,Cd,Cr,Cu,Hg,Pb}}$) computed
from the global mobility grid~\cite{qi2025}; six GeoROC bedrock
metals (log Co, Cr, Cu, Ni, Pb, Zn) from
\texttt{arkinlab\_microbeatlas.enriched\_metadata}; and Soil pH, ERA5
temperature, and Env PC1 (first PC of the eight metal+pH+temperature variables).

\subsection{Model configurations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{All-KO LOPO} (Step~1a): 118 KO densities + genome size;
        $15 \times 11 = 165$ fits. Metric: Spearman~$\rho$ on held-out genera.
  \item \textbf{Full-data SHAP}~\cite{lundberg2017} (Step~1b): CatBoost fit on all genera per
        response; SHAP via \texttt{get\_feature\_importance(type=`ShapValues')}.
  \item \textbf{Single-KO LOPO} (Step~1c): two features (KO density + genome
        size) per KO~$\times$ response~$\times$ fold; $\approx\!19{,}470$ fits
        total; faster hyperparameters (\texttt{iterations=100}).
  \item \textbf{Within-subset LOPO + SHAP} (Step~1d): four functional subsets
        (Resistance, Transport, Sensing, Cofactor) $\times$ 15 responses
        $\times$ 11 folds.
  \item \textbf{Functional-subset LOPO + SHAP heatmap} (Steps~2a/2b):
        12 functional-subset $z$-scores as features.
  \item \textbf{Abundance-weighted comparison} (Step~3): sample weights
        $\log_{10}(n_\text{samples}+1)$, normalised to mean~$= 1$.
  \item \textbf{Reverse classifier} (Step~4): \texttt{CatBoostClassifier}
        predicts KO presence (binary) from the 15 environmental $z$-scores;
        LOPO AUC.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{KO prioritisation composite score}

For each of 118 KOs, four sub-scores were computed and then min--max
normalised to $[0,1]$ across all 118 KOs:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $s_1$: best $|\overline{\rho}|$ across all 15 responses
        (peak predictive power).
  \item $s_2$: mean $|\overline{\rho}|$ across all 15 responses (breadth).
  \item $s_3$: count of responses with $|\overline{\rho}| \geq 0.10$.
  \item $s_4$: $-\log_{10}(\text{BH-FDR }q_\text{min})$ from the per-KO PGLS
        on the three responses with available data (GEOROC Co, GEOROC Pb,
        Soil pH); zero if no PGLS $p$ is available.
\end{enumerate}
Composite score $= (s_1 + s_2 + s_3 + s_4)/4$.
KEGG pathway annotations for the top~25 KOs were assigned from KEGG~\cite{kanehisa2023} database
entries; the four module groupings (Porphyrin/Cobalamin, Cu/Ag efflux cus,
Metal transport, Sensing/stress/other) reflect documented pathway membership.
Five KOs were designated as validation priorities:
K01772~(\textit{hemH}), K09883~(\textit{cobT}), K07796~(\textit{cusC}),
K19575~(\textit{bmrR}), K09823~(\textit{zur}).

\section{Social niche breadth benchmarking}
\label{sec:snb_benchmark}
% ============================================================================

$\text{SNB}_\text{SES}$ was computed for all 3{,}389 genera detected in
$\ge 10$ MicrobeAtlas samples (of 3{,}433 genera total; 462{,}716 samples
across the full MicrobeAtlas database). For each genus $i$ with $N_i$ occurrences, the
raw SNB partner count is the number of other genera co-occurring in at least
one shared sample. Because $S = 462{,}716$ makes the standard
999-permutation null model computationally infeasible, we used the exact
analytical null: the probability that genus $j$ is present in a random draw
of $N_i$ samples from $S$ is $p_{ij} = 1 - (1 - N_j/S)^{N_i}$, giving
null mean $\mu_i = \sum_{j \ne i} p_{ij}$ and null variance
$\sigma^2_i = \sum_{j \ne i} p_{ij}(1-p_{ij})$ (Bernoulli indicator sum,
exact as $S \to \infty$). SES $= (\text{raw} - \mu_i)/\sigma_i$.
Per-genus metrics and PGLS inputs are saved in
\texttt{data/snb\_von\_meijenfeldt\_replication.csv}.

Spearman correlations between $\text{SNB}_\text{SES}$ and our primary
co-occurrence and niche-breadth metrics are given in
Table~\ref{tab:snb_correlations}. PGLS regressions of $\text{SNB}_\text{SES}$
against metal-gene density (soil stratum, $n = 838$ genera, $\lambda = 0.81$)
gave: Model~1, total KO density: $\beta = -0.35$, $p = 0.49$; Model~2,
resistance vs.\ cofactor decomposition: $\beta_\text{resist} = +0.67$,
$p = 0.22$; $\beta_\text{cofactor} = +0.68$, $p = 0.27$. Neither model
reached significance, consistent with the interpretation that
$\text{SNB}_\text{SES}$ at this global scale reflects biogeographic range
rather than local metal-gene niche structure.

\section{Two-scale phylogenetic signal: method detail and validity assessment}
\label{sm:twoscale}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Method overview and novelty}

The two-scale framework combines genus-level Pagel's $\lambda$ (applied to continuous genus presence fractions) with genome-level Fritz~\&~Purvis D (applied to binary genome presence/absence) on the same set of 275 curated metal KOs.
This specific combination does not appear in the literature we searched; the closest conceptual analog pairs two distinct evolutionary signals (synteny index and sequence-mutability measure) to detect HGT between closely related taxa~\cite{adato2015}.
The general logic---that different methods detect transfers at different evolutionary timescales, with composition/signal-based methods sensitive to recent transfers and phylogenetic/topology-based methods to ancient fixed transfers---is established across the HGT-detection literature.
$\lambda$ here captures deep, genus-level phylogenetic clustering (trait conserved across millions of years of vertical transmission); D captures genome-level dispersal (recent or episodic horizontal acquisition detectable as phylogenetic randomness within and across genera).

\subsection{D computation: single pooled genome tree}

Fritz~\&~Purvis D was estimated once per KO on a single pooled 18,961-tip GTDB genome tree (all bacterial genomes in the dataset), not per-genus.
For each KO, binary presence/absence was coded across all 18,961 genome tips; the observed ancestral-state-change statistic was calibrated against Brownian-motion ($D = 0$ expectation) and random-permutation ($D = 1$ expectation) null distributions, each with $n = 1{,}000$ simulations.
This produces a single D value per KO, interpretable as a spectrum from phylogenetically clustered ($D \approx 0$, Brownian-motion-like) to phylogenetically dispersed ($D \approx 1$, random).

The choice of a pooled tree over per-genus trees is a deliberate design decision: it tests for genome-level randomness across the full phylogenetic depth of the dataset, capturing both deep and shallow dispersal, rather than per-genus dispersal which would require a separate tree and sufficient sample size for each genus.
The trade-off is that small genera (few genomes) may have limited power to contribute meaningful signal to the genome-scale D estimate; because D is computed on the pooled tree, this is less of a per-genus issue than for $\lambda$.

\subsection{Validity concern 1: Pagel's $\lambda$ on bounded presence-fraction data}

Pagel's $\lambda$ was designed for continuous traits evolving under Brownian motion (BM)---an unconstrained Gaussian process.
Genus presence fractions are bounded proportions in $[0, 1]$, violating the Gaussian/BM assumption in two specific ways:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Boundary compression.} Near-core genes (fraction $\to 1$) and rare genes (fraction $\to 0$) cannot vary freely; $\lambda$ estimation assumes residuals can take any real value, which is false at the boundaries.
Many genes in a curated 275-KO metal-gene set will fall near one boundary or the other (core cofactor genes near 1.0; rare resistance genes near 0.0), making this a particularly relevant concern.

\item \textbf{Unequal measurement precision.}
A genus with $n = 2$ genomes can only yield presence fractions of 0, 0.5, or 1 --- completely discrete --- while a genus with $n = 200$ genomes provides a continuous fraction with real precision.
Standard PGLS/$\lambda$ estimation does not downweight low-precision tips unless genome-count-weighted measurement error is explicitly modelled.
In the primary dataset, 308 of 1,574 genera have exactly two genomes ($n_\text{genomes} = 2$ minimum), potentially injecting discretisation noise at the tips.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Robustness checks (performed).}
We applied two checks to assess whether the bounded-proportion structure drives our $\lambda$ estimates.

\textit{Check A: Arcsine-square-root transformation (Python, $n = 88$ KOs).}
Genus presence fractions were arcsine-square-root-transformed before $\lambda$ estimation.
Re-estimating $\lambda$ for all 88 Tier~1--2 KOs with sufficient genus representation yielded Spearman $r = 0.613$ ($p < 0.001$) between original and transformed estimates (mean $|\Delta\lambda| = 0.195$; median $0.179$).
Nine KOs (10.2\%) crossed the $\lambda = 0.3$ weak/strong classification threshold, and \emph{all nine} changed in the weak-to-strong direction, indicating that the original PGLS \emph{underestimates} phylogenetic signal for sparse genes rather than overestimating it.
The shifts are concentrated in transport and resistance genes with very low original $\lambda$ (e.g., $mntC$: $0.000 \to 0.525$; $troA$: $0.143 \to 0.683$; $shp$: $0.000 \to 0.552$).
None of the 13 double-signal KOs (low $\lambda$ and high D) changed classification under this transformation.

\textit{Check B: Phylogenetic lambda model on transformed fractions (\texttt{phylolm} in R, $n = 33$ KOs).}
We fitted \texttt{phylolm} with \texttt{model="lambda"} to arcsine-square-root-transformed fractions for 33 KOs for which all required data were available.
Spearman $\rho = 0.174$ between original PGLS $\lambda$ and \texttt{phylolm} $\lambda$ (mean $|\Delta\lambda| = 0.281$; 10/33, 30.3\% classification changes), with all changes again in the weak-to-strong direction.
The lower concordance relative to Check~A likely reflects the smaller matched sample ($n = 33$ KOs) as much as methodological difference; the directional consistency confirms the conservative bias of the original estimates.

\textbf{Conclusion.}
Both transformation approaches reveal that original $\lambda$ estimates \emph{moderately underestimate} phylogenetic signal for sparse genes.
Because all classification shifts move weak-to-strong, our identification of cofactor KOs as high-$\lambda$ and resistance KOs as low-$\lambda$ is conservative: the 13 double-signal (low-$\lambda$, high-D) KOs (6 resistance, 4 transport, 1 sensing, 1 metabolism, 1 unknown; Table~\ref{tab:double_signal}) have at least as much phylogenetic clustering as stated.
Beta-regression alternatives (e.g., \texttt{betareg} in R) were not available in our compute environment and remain a priority for future independent replication.

\subsection{Validity concern 2: sparse-genus noise and the orthogonality result}

The Spearman correlation between D and $\lambda$ across 275 KOs is $\rho = -0.041$ ($p = 0.49$), reported in the main text as consistent with the two metrics capturing distinct evolutionary processes.
However, a near-zero correlation has two equally plausible interpretations:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{True independence:} D and $\lambda$ capture biologically distinct signals (dispersal timescale) and are genuinely orthogonal.
\item \textbf{Noise dominance:} One or both metrics are dominated by estimation variance (particularly for $\lambda$ estimated from sparse-genome genera or small-KO-presence KOs), and the near-zero $\rho$ reflects the absence of a detectable signal in either direction rather than biological independence.
\end{itemize}

Distinguishing these interpretations requires a threshold stability check: recompute $\rho$ after successively removing genera below minimum-genome-count thresholds ($n_\text{min} = 2, 5, 10, 20$); if $\rho$ remains near zero, sparse-genome genera are not masking a real correlation.

\textbf{Robustness check (performed).}
We built a complete genus~$\times$~KO presence matrix for 240 of the 275 metal KOs from 16~million genome records (MGnify MAGs and SPIRE prokaryotic genomes combined) and recomputed $\rho(\lambda, D)$ at each threshold.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{rrrrl}
\toprule
$n_\text{min}$ & Genera retained & KOs with data & $\rho$ & $p$ \\
\midrule
2  & 8,256 & 238 & $-0.068$ & 0.295 \\
5  & 3,799 & 237 & $-0.020$ & 0.765 \\
10 & 2,166 & 236 & $-0.097$ & 0.138 \\
20 & 1,204 & 232 & $+0.100$ & 0.129 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The range across thresholds is $\Delta\rho = 0.197$ and no threshold yields a significant correlation (all $p > 0.12$).
Importantly, $\rho$ does not trend monotonically toward a detectable signal as sparse genera are removed; the values oscillate near zero throughout.
This confirms that the near-zero $\rho$ is not an artefact of sparse-genome genera dominating either metric: when low-genome-count genera are progressively excluded, no latent correlation emerges.
The two-scale framework's orthogonality result is therefore robust, supporting the interpretation that $\lambda$ and D capture biologically distinct evolutionary signals at different timescales.

\subsection{Comparison with Panstripe}

Panstripe~\cite{tonkinhill2023} is a generalised-linear-regression-based tool designed specifically for phylogenetically controlled analysis of gene gain and loss rates in prokaryotic pangenomes.
It is robust to population structure, sampling bias, and errors in predicted presence/absence of genes---all concerns that apply to the D statistic as applied here.
Panstripe was not applied in this analysis primarily because it requires a per-gene presence/absence matrix with a shared pangenome phylogeny, whereas our D estimation used a pooled genome tree from GTDB.
Applying Panstripe to the 140 primary metal KOs and comparing gene-gain/loss rates between cofactor biosynthesis and resistance subcategories would provide a more rigorous, purpose-built alternative to the D-based HGT screen and is an explicit future priority.

\caption{\textbf{Coreness-matched permutation: the primary $\beta$ is not
distinctive relative to matched gene sets.}
Histogram of $\beta$ from 1,000 coreness-matched null KO sets ($n = 140$ KOs each,
matched to the primary set by pan-genome prevalence decile).  Red vertical line
marks the observed $\beta = -0.021$ (empirical $p = 0.298$).  Null distribution:
median $= -0.018$, SD $= 0.007$.  The primary metal-gene $\beta$ falls within the
null distribution, indicating that the streamlining signal is not statistically
unusual for a gene set of this size and prevalence distribution.}

Forest plot of PGLS $\beta$ for four phylum-stratified analyses
